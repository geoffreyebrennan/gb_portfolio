Product Practice

How I make product decisions and capture requirements

This is how I work, from a messy problem to a decision the team trusts and requirements engineers can build from. It draws on product work at CloudNC and Curve Dental, and on a design background that taught me to start with the person using the product.

AuthorGeoffrey Brennan
RoleSenior Product Manager
FocusDecisions & delivery
ApproachEvidence to outcomes
01 — Principles

The principles behind the work

  • Outcome first. Start from the outcome we want to move, not the feature someone asked for.
  • Evidence over opinion. Use more than one source, and say how strong the evidence is.
  • Match effort to reversibility. Decide reversible things quickly. Slow down for decisions that are hard to undo.
  • Requirements are shared understanding. A story is a way for the team to agree, not an instruction handed over.
  • Never let the product mislead. If data is missing, show it as missing, never as zero. Users act on what we show them.
  • Ship safely, then learn. Release the smallest safe slice, learn from real use, then extend.
02 — Product decisions

How I make product decisions

  1. Frame the decision. What outcome are we trying to move? Who is affected? What happens if we do nothing? When do we need to decide? I also classify the decision: reversible (decide fast, small group) or hard to reverse (more evidence, wider review).
  2. Gather evidence from several sources. Behavioural data (what users actually do), qualitative research (interviews, recorded customer calls), operational signals (error monitoring, support tickets) and commercial signals (pipeline, churn reasons). I note where sources agree and where they conflict.
  3. Generate options. At least two real options, including doing less or doing nothing.
  4. Weigh the options against four lenses. Customer value, technical feasibility, commercial opportunity and product risk. For close calls I use a simple score such as RICE or cost of delay, but the score informs the decision rather than making it.
  5. Decide, record and communicate. A short decision note: context, options, decision, reasons, what would change our mind, owner and date. I share it with engineering, design, sales and customer success so everyone hears the same reasoning.
  6. Set the signal and revisit. Before we build, agree the measure of success and a date to review it.

Worked example: CAM Assist 2.0 at CloudNC

StepWhat happened
FrameAdoption of our AI toolpaths was limited. The working assumption in the team was that users needed better documentation and hand-held onboarding.
EvidenceI compared toolpaths generated with those actually sent to the CAM system, and reviewed recorded calls, Sentry data and customer interviews. Users were generating AI toolpaths but hesitating to trust them.
HypothesisExpert machinists would not commit output they could not inspect, because a plausible-looking but wrong toolpath can scrap a part.
OptionsImprove documentation and onboarding, or redesign the product experience around visibility and user control.
DecisionRedesign: interactive 3D visualisation, real-time machinability feedback and inspectable workflow stages, so users could understand and challenge AI decisions before production.
OutcomeContributed to a 15% churn reduction on the previous quarter.

03 — Requirements

How I capture and write requirements

  1. Discover. I run workshops with users and stakeholders, observe the work where I can, and map the journey. I capture business rules, edge cases, constraints and the language users actually use.
  2. Write the problem statement. One paragraph on the problem, a measurable outcome, and explicit non-goals.
  3. Structure the work. Epics break into features, and features into stories small enough to finish in a sprint. Each story states the user, the need and the value.
  4. Make it testable. Acceptance criteria in Given / When / Then form, covering the happy path, edge cases and error states. I add non-functional needs (performance, accessibility, security, permissions, audit), data rules (source of truth, missing versus zero, freshness, rounding) and the analytics events we need.
  5. Agree the technical contract. Before refinement I sit with the tech lead to agree the interface: what goes in, what comes out, validation rules, error responses, who owns which state, what happens on a timeout or partial failure, and how we stay backwards compatible. We write down each trade-off and who made the call.
  6. Check readiness and manage change. Stories meet the Definition of Ready before a sprint and the Definition of Done before release. Changes are logged, and risks or delays are raised as soon as I see them.

Example story (illustrative)

As an account manager, I want to see each client’s monthly total, so that I can spot changes before our review meeting.

  • Given a client has complete data for the month, when I open the summary, then I see the total and the date it was last updated.
  • Given a client has no data for the month, when I open the summary, then I see “Not available” with the reason, and never “0”.
  • Given the data source is unavailable, when I open the summary, then I see a clear message and the last known figure, marked with its date.
  • Given I do not have permission for a client, when I search, then that client does not appear.

Real examples

  • Clearer requirements, less rework. At CloudNC I designed a hole-specification capability that reduced back-and-forth between requirements and implementation, improving the path from customer need to buildable requirement.
  • Requirements with business rules at their core. At Curve Dental I led Roles & Permissions, adding access control and separation-of-duties capabilities. It unlocked enterprise sales and contributed more than $2M in sales in its first quarter.
04 — Agile in practice

Agile that keeps the team moving toward outcomes

I have worked with five agile teams at CloudNC and six at Curve Dental. In practice:

  • Planning. A sprint goal tied to an outcome, not a list of tickets.
  • Stand-up. For unblocking, not status reporting.
  • Refinement. With the tech lead and designer, aiming for about two sprints of ready backlog. I split stories by slices of value, not by technical layer.
  • Backlog. Ordered rather than just prioritised. The top is small and ready; the bottom is rough on purpose.
  • Review. Demo working software to stakeholders and, where possible, customers.
  • Retrospective. Agree one change and follow it through.
  • Releases. MVPs and phased releases, with beta users recruited alongside Sales and Customer Success, and release messaging agreed with product marketing.
05 — Critical situations

When something breaks, respond with speed and clarity

When something breaks that customers depend on, such as a payroll run, speed and clear communication matter as much as the fix.

WhenWhat I do
First 15 minutesAcknowledge it. Assess impact: who is affected, how many, and whether money, data or a legal deadline such as a payroll cut-off is at risk. Set severity, open one channel and name an incident lead.
First hourContain it: roll back, switch off the feature or put a manual workaround in place. Tell affected customers what we know, what we are doing and when they will next hear from us. For high severity, update at least hourly.
ResolutionFix it, verify with the customer that the outcome is correct (for payroll, that people are paid the right amount on time), and close the loop.
AfterwardsA blameless review within days: root cause, what we will change, and prevention work in the backlog with owners and dates.
06 — Client & partner conversations

Own the conversation and the follow-through

  • Own it. I stay the point of contact rather than handing the conversation over.
  • Acknowledge and reassure. Show I understand the impact on their business.
  • Offer a workaround now. Give something that helps today, and a plan for the full fix.
  • Commit to an update. Be honest about what I don’t know and give a time for the next update.
  • Involve experts without stepping back. Bring in specialists when needed, while I keep ownership of the relationship.
07 — Learning a new domain

Start with the people, the work and the constraints

I learned manufacturing by talking to machinists, programmers and manufacturing leaders, and dental practice software by working through clinical and billing workflows with customers. In a new sector, especially a regulated one, my first weeks look like this:

  • Talk to users and to the people they serve, because the buyer, the user and the end client often want different things.
  • Map regulation, confidentiality and audit needs early with compliance and legal, and turn them into requirements.
  • Learn the vocabulary and shadow support to see real problems.
  • Write down my assumptions and test them before relying on them.
